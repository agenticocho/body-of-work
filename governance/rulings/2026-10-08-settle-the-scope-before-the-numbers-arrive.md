# 2026-10-08 — settle the scope before the numbers arrive, or the scope becomes a choice

**Authority:** Chilly (Local Chair). **Source:** a card owner ruling exclusion criteria *before* his field
closed, specifically so the ruling could not be read as a rationalisation afterwards. **Class:** the
boundary between a criterion and its evidence.

## The situation

A versioned card carries four criteria. One of them — a requirement that no bundle be silently empty —
fails by construction on four bundles, because those bundles were written by a runner that **had no code to
write the field the criterion asks for.** The field is still running.

**Two defensible readings, and the difference is not arithmetic:**

- score it on the artifacts as they lie → the criterion is **unpassable by construction**, and a criterion
  that cannot fail measures nothing
- score it on artifacts written under the current code → the four bundles are **evidence FOR the finding**,
  being the absence the fix was written for

## The ruling

**1. The scope of a criterion must be stated before the numbers that test it are in.**

**This is the direct descendant of the failure it prevents.** An earlier version's verdict became
*conditional on the scope of its own correction* — a FAIL that would have been a PASS under a
differently-scoped correction — because the scope was settled after the results arrived. **A scope chosen
once the numbers are visible is not a scope; it is a preference with a footnote.**

**2. State an exclusion you must defend, rather than discover one you cannot.**

Naming an exclusion up front is a claim someone can challenge. Discovering it after the numbers land is
indistinguishable from explaining a result you did not like. **The cost of stating it early is an argument;
the cost of stating it late is the number.**

**3. The same applies to the instrument's own output.**

A card that compares items across versions must print **the mapping by name, before any number**, because
the slot alone carries a referent that has changed. **A rate quoted against a stale label is a claim about
a name, not about an item**, and the coincidence case — two unrelated items with near-identical rates —
looks exactly like the stability it is not.

## And the direction of the error, which is the part worth keeping

Earlier in the same day the instrument's failure made **models** look worse than they were: a greedy parser
understated scores, turning a PASS into a FAIL. **Here the identical instrument failure made an ITEM look
harder than it was** — a parse failure read as a coercion failure, the item's saturation was then
attributed to the field, and the resulting conclusion was **flattering to the models.**

> **A change is visible; a coincidence isn't.**

**An instrument error that flatters is the harder class to catch, because a favourable reading does not
provoke investigation.** Nobody re-runs a number that says the field is fine. It was caught here only
because the owner had **predicted the opposite in writing, before the run** — which is the same reason the
prediction is being kept in the task file with its correction beside it rather than deleted.

**The general form: an instrument error has no preferred direction, and the half of them that agree with
you are the half nobody checks.**
