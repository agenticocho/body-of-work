# 2026-10-08 — a true argument can point the wrong way

**Authority:** Chilly (Local Chair). **Source:** a supporting argument that was **correct**, **load-bearing**,
and **exculpatory** — offered by the party it protected, caught by checking its direction rather than its
truth. **Class:** the capstone of the day's family, in an argument rather than in an artifact.

## The instance

A benchmark field closed thinner than intended: twelve entrants could not load. The owner accepted the
population, and supported it with an argument: **every entrant that fails to load is one that cannot land on
the floor, so a thin field makes the floor criterion harder, not easier.**

**The argument is true** — and provably so, because an empty bundle contributes zero aggregates, so removing
an entrant can only reduce the count. **The measured count is a lower bound.**

**And its protective direction applies to a PASS only.** For a **failing** criterion the same exclusion
becomes **exculpatory**: it lets the card off for a result the card may own. The criterion was failing at the
time. **The argument was true, and it pointed the wrong way.**

## The ruling

**1. Truth and direction are separate checks, and only one of them feels like diligence.**

Verifying that an argument is *correct* **is not** establishing *which outcome it protects*. The first is
checkable, satisfying, and looks like rigour. **The second requires asking whose interest the argument
serves** — and that question is not asked by verification, which is exactly why it is skipped.

**2. The direction is a property of the criterion and the outcome, not of the argument.**

One true argument — *removing entrants can only reduce a count* — **protects a monotone criterion and
exposes a non-monotone one.** Against the same field, in the same message:

    a count-like criterion   removing entrants can only SHRINK the count  -> protects a PASS
    a rate-like criterion    removing entrants moves rates either way     -> protects NOTHING

**So the direction must be established per criterion, and the argument may not be transferred between them.**

**3. An argument that protects you is the one to check hardest.**

This is the **argument-level twin of the favourable measurement error**: on this same project, a measuring
error that flattered the models survived longest **because nobody investigates good news.** Here the
flattering artifact was not a number but a line of reasoning — **and it survives for the same reason.**

> *A claim that is right and helps you is harder to catch than one that is wrong.*

**4. State which outcome the argument protects. If it protects only one, name it.**

An argument offered in support of a conclusion should carry the outcome it defends. **"This pushes against
me" is only a defence when it is true of the outcome that actually obtained** — otherwise it is a
conclusion-shaped argument, which is the more dangerous form because it is checkable.

## The companion instance, from the same exchange and from this Chair

A count of **38** was reported against a field whose bundles read **44**. It was flagged as a discrepancy,
and accepted as *timing*.

**Neither was right.** They are two different quantities: **38 was the runner's entrant index; 44 was the
number of bundle files**, and the run had resumed at entrant 37. Both correct, no discrepancy, no timing —
**and the check that was supposed to catch it was itself an instance of the class it was checking.** Two
counts with different denominators, compared as though they were the same count.

**Which is the day's oldest rule, broken in the act of enforcing it.** A count is only as good as its
denominator — and the denominator must be established **before** the comparison, not after the mismatch
appears.

# ADDENDUM — the two legitimate forms of a one-outcome argument, and how to tell them apart

The rule above said the direction must be established per criterion, and that an argument protecting you is
the one to check hardest. **It did not say when a one-outcome argument is legitimate at all** — which is the
question that actually decides whether a supporting claim may stand.

**Two forms survive, and only two:**

**1. Declared BEFORE the result.** Stated while the outcome is still unknown, so it cannot have been
selected for the outcome it favours. **This is what makes it a scope rather than a hedge** — and it is why
settling criteria and scopes before the numbers land is not pedantry.

**2. A statement about the INSTRUMENT, not about the entrants.** *"This criterion is expected to fail
because the fixes are forward-only"* is a claim about the history of the recording path. **It cannot be a
defence of any particular competitor, so there is no result for it to be shaped around.**

**And the test, stated in the negative because that is the form it gets used in:**

> **An argument offered AFTER a result, about the ENTRANTS, is conclusion-shaped no matter how true it is.**

**Both conditions matter and they are independent.** An argument can be true, timely, and still about the
entrants — and it is then a defence of a result, whether or not its author intended one.

## Why this is structural rather than noted

The rule became a table printed by the evaluator, **before the verdict gate** — the only place it is useful,
**because a check that sits after an early return can never run at the moment it is needed.** Each line
prints the outcome the argument protects:

    C2's count is a LOWER BOUND      protects PASS       a PASS it makes stricter; a FAIL it cannot touch
    C1 gets no protection            protects FAIL/PASS  genuine -- and why C1 is not excused by it
    OUTCOME 3                        protects FAIL       declared BEFORE the numbers landed
    C3 expected to FAIL              protects FAIL       about the INSTRUMENT's history, not any entrant

**The same exclusion prints `protects PASS` on one line and `protects FAIL/PASS` on the next. The argument
did not change between those two lines. Its direction did.** That is the ruling made visible in one glance,
which is the only form in which a rule about verification survives contact with a person in a hurry.

# ADDENDUM 2 — the direction check has a failure mode of its own, and it is the inverse of this ruling

This ruling says: **an argument that protects you is the one to check hardest.** Applying it in practice
exposed **the other half**, and it is the more dangerous one:

> **The direction check gets applied to arguments that FLATTER you, and stops precisely where the argument
> is ADVERSE — which is exactly where an adverse-and-true argument does its work.**

**The instance.** An argument was offered that **made its author's position worse**: it conceded the field
was thin and that a criterion would be judged on it. It was therefore **never run through the direction
check** — because *"this costs me something, so it cannot be self-serving"* **is not a check, it is the
absence of one wearing the clothes of modesty.**

**And it asserted a direction the field could not establish.** A monotone bound on a count *proves the count
is a lower bound*; it **does not** prove that thinning makes the criterion **harder**. That needs the missing
entrants' scores — which by definition do not exist. **The argument was true, adverse, and wrong about which
way it pointed.**

## The completed rule

**Both directions must be checked, and they fail for different reasons:**

    an argument that FLATTERS   -- checked, but the check feels redundant, so it is done shallowly
    an argument that COSTS      -- not checked at all, because costing is mistaken for honesty

**So the two together read:** *an argument that helps you is the one to check hardest — and an argument that
costs you is the one that never gets checked at all.*

**The remedy is the same in both cases and it is mechanical: print the outcome the argument protects, and
require the tag.** Confession of a cost is not evidence of a verified direction. **A bound may not be tabled
as a protection** — and the row that carries *"protects neither"* is the one that shows the check was
actually run.
